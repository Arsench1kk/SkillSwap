# SkillSwap Assignment 5 Product Strategy and Differentiation

Working draft for the 15–20 slide presentation and evidence appendix.

Team: Togzhan, Amir, Nurlan, Ruslan, Andrey, Arsen Abduhalyk  
Geography: Kazakhstan, starting with Almaty and Astana  
Planning horizon: 12 weeks  
Evidence labels: `R` = respondent evidence, `S` = published source, `P` = peer/workshop feedback, `H` = hypothesis

## Core recommendation

SkillSwap should focus first on university students in Almaty and Astana who face a specific STEM or creative-software roadblock before a deadline. The 12-week strategy is to prove a trusted campus peer-help loop: a student submits a concrete task, receives a suitable peer match within 24 hours, completes a structured session and reports whether the session helped.

The product should remain free by default through skill barter. A paid session credit should be treated as a controlled monetisation experiment only after the free matching loop works. This direction is supported by the existing student interviews and the team's access to course chats and student clubs, but matching speed, mentor supply, repeat use and willingness to pay remain untested `H`.

## Slide 1 — Assignment 4 recap

**Customer:** University students and self-learners in Kazakhstan who get stuck on a specific task in video editing, higher mathematics, programming or languages `R1–R3`.

**JTBD:** When I am stuck on a specific task, connect me with a verified peer who solved it, so I can get tailored guidance for free `R1–R3`.

**Strongest alternative:** Free AI/video resources plus classmates and course chats: free and immediate, but generic or limited by the student's existing circle `R1–R3`.

**Reason to switch — current hypothesis:** SkillSwap finds a trusted peer outside the student's immediate circle who understands the exact task, provides a structured session and keeps barter free by default `H`.

**Chosen business model:** Free barter first; pay-per-session credit only when no suitable swap is available `H`.

**First channel:** Faculty Telegram/WhatsApp study chats and two student clubs `R1, R2, H`.

**Riskiest assumption:** Students will accept and complete a session with an unfamiliar peer, and some learners will pay for a credit when barter fails `H`.

## Slide 2 — Evidence-based strategic question

### Three insights

1. **Free tools are the first choice, but they fail on specific context.** R1 tried YouTube, Gemini and student chats for an After Effects task; generic instructions did not match the project setup and chat responses were slow `R1`.
2. **Peer help is already socially acceptable when trust and reciprocity exist.** R2 teaches friends for free because explaining reinforces her own learning, but finding advanced expertise outside the immediate circle is difficult `R2`.
3. **Competence alone is not enough; the session must feel trustworthy and structured.** R3 stopped informal exchange because sessions felt awkward and unstructured and asked for ratings, achievements, portfolios or shared interests `R3`.

### Strategic question for the next 12 weeks

> Can a campus-first, structured peer-matching loop help students complete specific roadblocks reliably enough to create repeat use, before we broaden the segment or depend on paid credits?

## Slide 3 — Product vision and priority outcome

### Product vision

Students should be able to overcome a difficult learning or project roadblock without being limited by money or by the skills of their immediate circle. SkillSwap should make trusted, reciprocal peer help easy to find, easy to start and safe to use.

### Priority customer outcome

The priority student completes a useful peer session for a specific task before the deadline, with less waiting, uncertainty and frustration than the current AI/chat workaround.

This is intentionally narrower than “improve education” or “build a global learning community.”

## Slide 4 — Two strategic directions

| Criterion | Direction A: Campus-first deadline support | Direction B: Broad recurring skill exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | University students in Almaty/Astana with urgent STEM or creative-software tasks | Students plus lifelong learners; regular language and skill practice |
| Customer value | Fast, trusted help for one concrete roadblock | Broad access to regular peer learning |
| Advantage over alternatives | Extends the trusted circle while preserving free barter and adding structure | Breadth, convenience and potentially recurring premium access |
| Feasibility | Existing student interviews, campus access, course chats and clubs support a small pilot `R1, R2, H` | Evidence outside students is weak; supply and recurring demand are unknown `H` |
| Trade-off | Smaller initial market and lower expected early revenue | Larger scope but weaker focus and greater marketplace complexity |
| Decision | **Selected** | **Deferred** |

Direction A is selected because it has stronger evidence and a more reachable first channel. Its trade-off is acceptable for a 12-week learning cycle.

## Slide 5 — Selected direction and strategic boundary

### Strategy statement

> We will focus on Kazakhstan university students who need one-time help with a specific task before a deadline, create value through fast trusted peer guidance, win against free AI plus classmates by extending the trusted circle with structured matching, and prioritise a campus-first free-barter model with a paid fallback credit within 12 weeks.

### In scope

- Almaty and Astana university students.
- STEM and creative-software tasks first.
- Course chats and student clubs as initial channels.
- Manual or lightweight matching before full automation.
- Useful session completion and trust as primary evidence.

### Two non-priorities

1. **Lifelong learners and broad international expansion.** Opportunity cost: less time to prove the student use case and campus supply loop.
2. **Subscription-first monetisation and a large feature build.** Opportunity cost: slower revenue learning, but it protects the free/barter behaviour that may create mentor supply `R2, H`.

## Slide 6 — Differentiation against alternatives

| Alternative | What it does well | Where SkillSwap can be different |
|---|---|---|
| Free AI + YouTube | Immediate, free generic guidance `R1` | Human context for the exact setup, peer accountability and a structured session `H` |
| Classmates and course chats | Trusted, free and familiar `R1, R2` | Reach beyond the immediate circle while retaining visible competence and reciprocity `H` |
| Preply / paid tutors | Profiles, reviews, trial and structured paid help `S1, S3` | Free barter first and no recurring 28-day plan for an episodic need `H` |

### Current differentiator

Task-specific matching + visible trust signals + structured peer session + free barter first.

### Durable advantage status

No durable advantage is established. AI matching, ratings and session templates can be copied. A possible future advantage is a campus trust network with verified peer histories and local skill supply, but that is an untested `H`.

## Slide 7 — Competitor response and strategic risk

### If Preply copies free or low-cost peer matching

Preply could copy matching and trust features quickly. SkillSwap would still need to win through campus access, reciprocal skill credits, local course context and a lower commitment for urgent one-off tasks `H`.

### If AI assistants improve task-specific help

The “AI is generic” argument becomes weaker. SkillSwap must protect the human value: peer accountability, contextual explanation, visible competence and a completed session.

### If no peer is available

The product fails at its core promise. This is why matching speed and mentor supply are more important early indicators than registrations or feature completion.

## Slide 8 — Business-model fit and canvas updates

### Business-model fit

- **User:** learner student or peer mentor.
- **Buyer/payer:** learner student in the current model.
- **Charging unit:** one 60-minute fallback credit, not a monthly subscription `H`.
- **Payment timing:** only when barter cannot produce a suitable match `H`.
- **Channel:** course chats and clubs reach the student directly `R1, R2, H`.
- **Value conflict:** charging too early could reduce the free peer-help behaviour that creates supply.

## Slide 9 — Impact Map: goal, actors and behaviour changes

### WHY — business goal

Demonstrate a repeatable campus peer-help loop in which students complete useful task-specific sessions and the platform can later test sustainable funding.

### WHO — actors

- Learners with an urgent task.
- Peer mentors with a relevant skill.
- Student clubs and course-chat administrators who influence reach and trust.

### HOW — behaviour changes

- Learners submit a concrete task instead of stopping at generic AI or an unanswered chat.
- Mentors accept and complete sessions outside their immediate friend circle.
- Learners return for another useful task when a new roadblock occurs.
- A learner buys a fallback credit only after a suitable free swap cannot be found.

### WHAT — candidate initiatives

1. Concierge Midterm Swap Week through course chats and student clubs.
2. Structured trust and session package.
3. Mentor ambassador and supply-building campaign.
4. Paid fallback-credit experiment.

## Slide 10 — Initiative selection and weakest link

### Select for the next 12 weeks

**Initiative 1: Concierge Midterm Swap Week**  
Why: tests acquisition, demand, manual matching and channel reach with low build effort `R1, R2, H`.

**Initiative 2: Structured trust and session package**  
Includes skill evidence, availability, session template, ratings and no-show handling. Why: directly addresses R3's trust and structure concern and tests whether strangers will accept sessions `R3, H`.

### Defer

**Initiative 3: Mentor ambassador campaign**  
Defer until the pilot shows where supply is missing. Otherwise the team may optimise an assumed bottleneck `H`.

**Initiative 4: Paid fallback-credit experiment as a major launch initiative**  
Defer as a primary focus until the free loop creates useful sessions. A small payment probe may still be run after a failed barter attempt `H`.

### Weakest causal assumption

> Visible competence and a structured session will make mentors willing to accept unfamiliar learners and complete the exchange.

Weakening observation: good matching but low mentor acceptance or low session completion.

## Slide 12 — Acquisition, engagement and conversion signals

| Category | Metric definition | Segment / window | Warning decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | New eligible learner request: a first task request from a target student through a pilot channel | Target students; weekly | If requests are low, revise channel message or target cohort before building more features |
| Engagement | Accepted match that starts a structured session | Learner–mentor match; within 7 days of request | If low, inspect trust, availability, task clarity and mentor response time |
| Conversion | Eligible request that becomes a completed session within 7 days | Each eligible request; 7-day window | If low, limit acquisition and fix supply/matching before scaling |

## Slide 13 — Retention and monetisation signals

| Category | Metric definition | Segment / window | Warning decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retention | A participant who completed a first useful session submits or accepts another relevant request | First useful-session cohort; 30 days | If low, interview returning and non-returning users; do not assume the product lacks value without checking task frequency |
| Monetisation | Eligible unmatched request followed by a paid fallback-credit purchase | Requests with no suitable free match; 7 days | If zero or harmful to free participation, test sponsor funding or keep the product fully free |

Retention is based on a meaningful first useful session, not generic “30-day active users.” Segment retention by learner/mentor role, acquisition channel, task type and free/paid path.

## Slide 14 — Primary metric and guardrails

### Primary success metric

**Completed useful sessions per eligible learner request.**

Definition: number of eligible requests that result in a completed session rated as meaningfully helpful divided by total eligible requests in the same cohort and time window.

### Guardrail 1

**24-hour matching rate.** The share of eligible requests receiving an accepted suitable match within 24 hours. Provisional warning threshold: below 30% for two consecutive pilot weeks `H`.

### Guardrail 2

**Session usefulness/trust rating.** The share of completed sessions receiving a non-negative usefulness and trust rating. Provisional warning threshold: below 70% `H`.

### False improvement risk

The primary metric could improve if the team manually selects easy cases or counts highly motivated users only. Guardrails, cohort breakdowns and an audit of rejected/unmatched requests should reveal this.

## Slide 15 — Conflicting signals exercise

### Given pattern

Acquisition rises, engagement rises, conversion is unchanged, retention falls and monetisation rises.

### Explanation A — paid urgency, poor delivered value

Urgent learners are entering and paying, but first sessions are not useful enough to create return behaviour. Inspect paid versus free usefulness ratings, no-shows, time-to-match and retention by first-session outcome.

### Explanation B — low-quality broad acquisition plus a small paying niche

A small high-intent cohort is generating revenue while broad campaign traffic produces one-off engagement. Inspect cohort size, channel quality, revenue concentration, task type and repeat usage by segment.

### Unknowns

Retention may fall because of poor value, resolved one-time needs, low future task frequency or mentor supply problems. Aggregate metrics cannot distinguish these causes.

## Slide 16 — Action and revision conditions

### Action now

Limit the paid-credit initiative while inspecting completed-session usefulness and cohort retention. Continue the campus pilot and structured sessions only where the guardrails hold.

### Reconsider the wider strategy if

1. Less than 30% of eligible requests are matched within 24 hours for two consecutive pilot weeks `H`.
2. Paid users show materially lower usefulness or retention than free users, suggesting monetisation damages the core value `H`.

If either condition occurs, test a narrower task segment, stronger mentor supply intervention or a university-sponsored/fully free model before expanding.

Proceed with a campus-first, student-focused strategy for 12 weeks.
